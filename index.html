An open 0.8B model plays ten browser games from raw pixels.

PlayJev is Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base, fine-tuned until one forward pass turns a game frame into a probability over the moves. It sees the picture, not the game's internal state, and it writes nothing: the answer is read straight off the option letters, one decision every 43 ms on a single GPU.

One model plays all ten games, and the game's name never reaches it.

Every board here is a recorded episode replaying inside the real game. The bars are the probabilities the model gave that frame, and the move it played is the one in blue.

All ten at once

Ten games, one set of weights, ten different sets of moves to choose from. The label under each board says which recording is playing.

1x is the game's own pace. Ten live games is heavy on a laptop; one at a time keeps a single game running and leaves the others as names you can click.

How it works

The model sees the frame and the option list, rendered with the frozen OpenJev prompt, and its answer is the next token after "Answer:". The probability over moves is the softmax over the option letters at that position: one forward pass, no sampling, nothing generated. Confidence is (pmax − 1/K) / (1 − 1/K), so 0 is a uniform guess and 1 is certainty.

The most probable move is played, with one exception: a move that leaves the picture unchanged, such as a blocked direction in 2048 or Sokoban, is not repeated on that same picture, and the next most probable move goes instead.

Each board is the vendored game running with the shim and hook used in training, a virtual clock, a seeded random source and synthetic key events, so the recorded action sequence reproduces the episode step for step. The page checks the replayed score against the recording at the end of every episode and marks the board in coral if the two ever differ.
